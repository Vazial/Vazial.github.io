Case study · the method correcting itself

The rule broke, and the rule caught it

Every time development friction occurs — the AI hesitating, going the wrong way, or an instruction turning out to be ambiguous — it is supposed to be written down on the spot. The principle is right, and in practice the entries do not all get written. So the session logs get scanned for the moments worth reading, and the candidates come back for review. The first thing the loop surfaced was one of my own rules failing for the fourth time.

Source · 64 friction entries across four ledgers · decisions recorded in ADR-0049 and ADR-0055

Why write it down at all

A friction entry is not a bug report. It records the moment something went wrong, the hypothesis for why, and — the part that makes it worth the trouble — where the fix was pushed down to. A recurring cause is treated as a structural defect in the rules, not as bad luck, and the fix is a rule change rather than a reminder to be careful.

That only works if the entries exist. Writing them in the moment is correct, because reconstructing them later loses the detail. But "we did not write it down" was never meant to imply "it is gone forever", and the ledger had visible holes.

Three properties of the raw logs that make naive counting wrong

Before anything could be scanned, the logs had to be normalised, because measuring them directly produces confident nonsense. Three things were measured and folded:

Property Measured Why it matters
Sessions fork and resume over a fifth of all records are duplicates a week with more branching gets a bigger denominator, inventing a trend
Role agents write separate files 108 runs, comparable in size to the main log the friction inside delegated work is invisible from the main transcript
role: user is not a person 231 notifications against 522 actual human messages tool results counted as human speech

The third one bites hardest. Inside a role agent's log, role: user is the orchestrator briefing it — so counting naively turns my instruction to an agent into a human correction, which is close to the opposite of what it means. That happened once before it was caught.

Normalisation happens in exactly one place, and every tool above it reads that index rather than the raw files. Rebuilding it from scratch takes about two seconds, so it is rebuilt every run instead of being updated incrementally.

What the loop found that reading could not

Once the analysis ran over the normalised index rather than the main transcript, two things showed up that had never been visible:

  • An agent stuck on the same error 23 times in a row — the architect failing on a wrong working directory, over and over, inside a delegated run nobody was watching.
  • A silent rework: the orchestrator telling a role agent "my previous instruction to you was wrong" and restarting it. Real friction, never logged, because from the outside the slice just took longer.

And across sessions rather than within them:

File does not exist … working directory occurred 124 times across 14 sessions, and appeared zero times in a ledger of 51 entries.

Inside any single session that reads as "a few errors happened". Only the cross-session view makes it a defect.

The rule that broke

The entry the loop is named for. A person told me the architecture decision records had become hard to read — that you could not understand one on its own, and the prose had gone stiff.

Rather than argue about taste, the 49 records in scope were measured:

ADR-0001 to 0021 ADR-0022 to 0052
Bold text as a share of body 6.3% 15.5%
Parenthetical asides per document 0.7 4.8

The cause was not carelessness. An earlier rule already said that anything a human approves must open with a summary for the approver — and when that rule was written, a table of exceptions judged ADRs to be already compliant, because their decision section was serving as the conclusion. That judgement quietly stopped being true.

It was the fourth occurrence of the same cause key, and the first three were logged against a different project's ledger. The recurrence was only visible because the ledgers share cause keys across all four.

The fix went into the rules: records must stand alone, and the three stylistic properties a machine can actually count — bold ratio, longest sentence, number of asides — are now reported by the linter.

When it runs, and why not at merge

The scan triggers just before a pull request is opened, not after it merges.

Reflection belongs at the end of a piece of work, and opening the PR is the one moment in this workflow where a slice is deliberately closed and summarised. Merging is the wrong moment for a duller reason: once the PR is merged, recording the friction needs a second pull request — and "recording something requires another PR" is itself the most frequently recurring entry in the ledger. A harvesting mechanism that generates the friction it is trying to capture is not much of a mechanism.

The measured trigger rates agreed: gh pr create fired 98 times in 30 days, while gh pr merge fired 12 times in one — merges mostly happen through the web interface, where no local hook can see them.

What it cannot do

The honest limit, and it does not go away with more effort.

The obvious question is whether the loop makes things better, and the flow of work is too small to answer it. The correction rate — human messages that rewrite a previous instruction — currently sits around 3.8%, from roughly 75 prompts a week. Detecting a halving of that with any confidence needs about 1,200 prompts on each side of the comparison: something like seven and a half months of work before the question can be answered at all.

So the tooling does not claim improvement. It says not yet decidable and reports how many more observations it needs. What it can detect on this volume is a large regression — roughly a doubling, in about three months.

Measuring a thing does not entitle you to a conclusion about it, and a dashboard that implies otherwise is worse than no dashboard. The output here is candidates for a person to read, not a verdict.
